If you are new to the world of incense, the two woods you will hear about most often are agarwood and sandalwood. Both are among the most treasured aromatic materials in Asian incense traditions, yet they could hardly be more different in origin, scent, and price.

This guide compares them side by side — how each forms, what they smell like, what they cost, and which one is right for your practice.

## The Short Answer

| | Agarwood (*chenxiang* / oud) | Sandalwood (*tanxiang*) |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Resin formed in wounded *Aquilaria* trees | Heartwood of *Santalum* trees |
| **Aroma** | Complex, sweet-balsamic, medicinal, layered | Creamy, soft, warm, steady |
| **Rarity** | Extremely rare; only 2–10% of wild trees produce quality resin | Rare but more abundant; slow-growing |
| **Price** | Among the most expensive materials on earth — once worth more than gold | Expensive, but a fraction of agarwood's price |
| **Best for** | Meditation, special occasions, connoisseurship | Daily burning, blending, spiritual practice |
| **Conservation** | CITES Appendix II; wild trees endangered | CITES Appendix II; heavily regulated |

## How They Form: Wound vs. Heartwood

Agarwood is not the natural state of its tree. It begins with species of the *Aquilaria* genus — tall evergreens native to Southeast Asia and southern China. A healthy *Aquilaria* tree produces plain, nearly odorless wood. Only when the tree is wounded — by lightning, insects, or fungal infection — does it defend itself by secreting a dense, fragrant resin that gradually permeates the heartwood over years or decades. This is the agarwood we burn. As covered in [our guide to agarwood](/blog/agarwood-king-of-incense/), this defense mechanism is why agarwood is so extraordinarily rare: only an estimated 2–10% of wild *Aquilaria* trees produce resin of commercial quality.

Sandalwood forms differently. The fragrant heartwood of the *Santalum album* tree (and related species) develops naturally as the tree matures — no wounding required. Sandalwood is a hemiparasite: its roots attach to host trees and draw nutrients from them, which limits where it can be cultivated and keeps supply constrained. The tree must grow for 15–30 years before its heartwood develops the rich, creamy fragrance that makes it prized.

## Aroma Profiles: Complexity vs. Constancy

**Agarwood** offers one of the most complex fragrance profiles in the natural world. When gently heated, it unfolds in distinct layers:

- **Top notes**: a cool, medicinal opening with hints of camphor and citrus
- **Heart notes**: warm, sweet balsamic notes of vanilla, honey, and dark chocolate
- **Base notes**: deep, resinous, and woody with a faint animalic undertone that lingers for hours

A single piece of aged agarwood can shift its character continuously over a multi-hour session, revealing new facets as different resin compounds volatilize at different temperatures.

**Sandalwood**, by contrast, is a study in steadiness. Its signature is a creamy, milky-sweet woodiness that is warm, soft, and grounding — with subtle powdery undertones. It does not demand attention the way agarwood does; it sits quietly in the background, which is exactly why it has been a staple of meditation practice for centuries.

## Price and Rarity

There is no contest when it comes to cost. Agarwood is routinely sold by the gram, and top-grade sinking agarwood (*chenxiang*) can command prices comparable to gold. Its rarity — combined with the fact that wild *Aquilaria* trees are endangered across their natural range — keeps supply vanishingly small.

Sandalwood is expensive compared to most woods, but it costs a fraction of agarwood. A quality sandalwood stick or chip is an accessible daily luxury, while agarwood is typically reserved for special occasions.

Both materials are listed under CITES Appendix II, meaning international trade is regulated to protect wild populations. For a deeper look at how to judge quality, see [understanding agarwood grades and quality](/blog/understanding-agarwood-grades/).

## Which One Should You Choose?

**Choose sandalwood if you:**
- Want a daily incense that is calming and unobtrusive
- Practice meditation and want a steady, grounding scent
- Are blending incense and need a reliable base note
- Have a modest budget

**Choose agarwood if you:**
- Seek the deepest, most complex fragrance experience
- Want incense for special occasions and ceremonies
- Are a collector or connoisseur
- Value rarity and tradition over economy

## The Bottom Line

Agarwood and sandalwood are not competitors but complements. Sandalwood is the steady, accessible foundation of Chinese incense practice; agarwood is its rare and transcendent peak. Many practitioners keep both — sandalwood for daily burning, agarwood for the moments that call for something extraordinary.
